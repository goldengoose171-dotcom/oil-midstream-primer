WTI settlement, 20 April 2020. No war started. No refinery blew up. Supply and demand were unchanged from the Friday before. Traders paid strangers nearly $40 a barrel to take oil off their hands.

The number on your screen isn't "the price of oil." It's the price of oil somewhere specific, net of everything standing between the barrel and its buyer: ships, tanks, pipes, insurance paperwork, and permission. This primer distills how that middle layer actually works, and why it's where the money lands when the world breaks.

Part 1

The Day Oklahoma Broke the World Price

Spring 2020: demand had cratered by ~20m barrels a day while the Saudis and Russians flooded the market. But cheap oil doesn't explain a negative price. What does is a tank farm in Cushing, Oklahoma.

A WTI futures contract is just a number on a screen — until expiry day, when whoever holds it has promised to take physical delivery of 1,000 barrels at Cushing. By 17 April, roughly 60m of Cushing's 76m barrels of working storage were full, and every remaining gallon was leased or spoken for. A crowd of paper longs woke up holding delivery obligations in a town with no room.

Brent settled positive that same afternoon. Deferred WTI contracts never went negative. Nothing was wrong with oil. The middle was full.

Part 2

The Cost Ladder

Every barrel sits on a ladder of deliverable prices. It's worth one price if it can reach a benchmark-quality buyer, less if it can only go regional, less still if it's stuck behind a full pipeline, and nothing if it can't legally or physically move. Barrels always seek the cheapest machine with spare capacity — and when that machine fills, the price gap widens until the next, more expensive machine turns on.

Barrels flow to the cheapest machine with capacity. When it fills, the differential widens.

One law, and it explains Alberta's $40 discounts, Waha's negative gas, and every regional blowout in modern history. Tap each rung:

  • Humanity's cheapest way to move oil on land. Batches of different grades run in sequence down the same pipe. Capacity is sold two ways: committed shippers on take-or-pay contracts, and walk-up shippers at posted tariffs. When nominations exceed capacity, the pipe goes into apportionment — the single most information-dense word in the pipeline world. An apportioned pipe is a full rung.
  • At the waterline the machines become mobile. A VLCC carries ~2m barrels for long hauls; Suezmax ~1m; Aframax ~700k for shorter runs and shallower ports — and, not coincidentally, the shadow fleet's favorite size. Key one-way door: a clean product tanker can switch to dirty crude easily, but going back requires costly cleaning and re-certification. A 15-year-old tanker can be commercially dead to the mainstream market and simultaneously the hottest asset in the sanctioned one.
  • Two businesses in one steel shell: cycling (throughput fees on barrels passing through) and carrying (renting time to contango traders). Below working capacity sits the heel — the minimum fill needed to blend and push barrels out at rate. Tank tops break the market (April 2020). Tank bottoms strain it with basis stress and squeeze risk (Cushing near 20m bbls in June 2026). Storage is ultimately a trust business — somebody has to occasionally open the door and count.
  • A multiple of pipeline cost — the overflow valve. Crude-by-rail volume is a direct public gauge of how badly the cheaper rungs have filled. When you see railcars full of bitumen, you're watching the ladder law execute in public: the differential widened until the expensive machine switched on.
  • The dearest rung, and the rate card understates it. A road is a living organism: dust suppression, self-funded repairs, and relationships with everyone who lives along the route. A community that closes the road has removed a rung from your ladder without touching a machine. The bottom layer of the permission stack is consent.

Tolls vs. Options — the category error that costs money

Midstream assets earn in two modes. A toll is a contracted pipeline or take-or-pay terminal: volume × fee, indifferent to oil price, valued like infrastructure debt. An option is a spot tanker, an uncommitted tank, a flexible book: worth little in calm markets and worth anything in broken ones. Confusing the two is an expensive mistake — tanker equities are effectively listed call options, which is why they look cheap at every top and broken at every bottom. Buying them off a P/E screen is the classic trap.

Part 3

Space, Time, Form

Everything in the middle gets paid for closing one of three gaps: where a barrel is vs. where it's wanted, when it exists vs. when it's needed, and what it is vs. what the buyer's equipment can digest. A VLCC, a salt cavern, and a diluent contract are the same business pointed at different gaps. (A fourth gap — barrels that may legally move vs. those that may not — is the sanctions trade.)

Freight & tariffs. Tanker freight is quoted in Worldscale points — a taxi meter recalibrated once a year. WS100 is the published flat rate for a route; WS310 (where Gulf-to-China fixed in late June 2026) means the meter is running at 3.1× its calibrated norm. What the owner actually earns is the TCE: gross freight minus bunkers, port and canal fees, divided by round-trip days — including the empty ballast leg home.

A route is open when the price gap between two locations exceeds the cost of covering the distance. That single sentence is the entire theory of oil arbitrage.

THE TRADE — see a $2/bbl spread between two ports?
Anyone can see it on a screen. Only someone with a chartered ship and a berth slot can turn it into cash.

Storage & the curve. The futures curve is the published price of time. In contango (deferred above spot) the market pays anyone who can warehouse the surplus: buy spot, sell the deferred future, store, deliver. In backwardation (spot above deferred) storage earns nothing and inventories drain.

Spring 2020 blew the spreads out past tank rent, past financing, until they covered the most expensive warehouse on earth: VLCCs hired to sit still. Floating storage peaked near 200m barrels. Euronav booked $485m of net profit in H1 2020; Vitol cleared $3bn for the year — the same event that printed −$37.63, seen from the winning side.

THE SIGNAL — the moment floating storage starts building, the contango has already blown past the marginal onshore tank. The surplus is worse than the official data admits.

Blending & the spec cliff. A crude grade or refined product is a pass-fail exam. Marine fuel at 0.50% sulphur is a compliant commodity; at 0.51% it's a different, cheaper product with a smaller buyer pool. Price doesn't slope across that boundary — it steps.

Canada's bitumen is too viscous to pipe at all, so every barrel of Western Canadian Select embeds a form trade: ~30% purchased condensate. And IMO 2020 — one UN regulation about smokestacks — repriced a refining stream, a blending complex, and the relative value of every large ship on earth overnight.

THE TRADE — buy the failing stream at its discount, buy a correcting stream, blend in a tank until it passes, sell at the compliant price. Done daily at scale in Rotterdam, Fujairah, Singapore.
There was no directional bet. Trading houses don't get rich guessing whether oil goes up or down — flat price is the most competitive, most arbitraged market on earth. They spend a decade paying premiums on physical options (ships, tanks, terminals, prepay financing — billions a year of committed cost), then all the options strike at once when a chokepoint tightens. The house doesn't have to be right. It has to be present, insured, and staffed.
Part 4

Read the Curve

The curve's shape is the market's own signed daily statement about whether the physical present is heavy or tight. Toggle between the two regimes:

Contango: too much oil today. Deferred months trade above spot, and the curve is offering to pay anyone who can warehouse the surplus. If the spread beats storage + financing costs, the profit is locked the day both legs go on.

The price is manufactured, not discovered

"Brent" is assembled daily by named organizations out of a small set of physically deliverable cargoes under written eligibility rules. The North Sea stream now loads so little that Texas cargoes delivered into Rotterdam are netted back to a notional North Sea price using a published freight adjustment — the world's oil reference literally contains a tanker assessment as a term in its equation.

When war shut Hormuz in March 2026, Platts suspended any Dubai-deliverable grade requiring strait transit. The pool collapsed from five grades to two — Oman and Murban, the two that load outside the strait. Benchmark eligibility followed the bypass map. Dubai printed a record $157.66, and some Asian refiners re-papered purchases off ICE Brent because their benchmark had become unusable.

Methodology risk is market risk. A grade admitted to a benchmark gains a structural bid; a terminal struck off strands the barrels behind it. In March 2026, two subscriber notes from a price-reporting agency arguably moved more money than any OPEC statement of the year. Almost nobody reads methodology notices. Read them.
Part 5

The Chokepoints

Sort them by substitutability, not size. Malacca is the biggest by volume but has workarounds. Hormuz has ~4.7m bbl/d of pipeline bypass against a 20.9m flow and no maritime alternative at all — which is why one strait carries the world's risk premium. Tap a bar for detail. Magenta = elevated risk in the 2026 crisis.

  • The largest chokepoint on earth — not Hormuz. Closure wouldn't be existential: Indonesian straits and the Myanmar pipeline offer workarounds. It carries China's nightmares more than the market's premium.
  • The largest by irreplaceability: ~4.7m bbl/d of pipeline bypass, no sea alternative. 89% of its crude goes to Asia. In the 2026 war, transits collapsed from ~135 tanker passages a day to under 10. The technically correct definition of the Hormuz premium: the cost of not owning a pipeline to Fujairah.
  • Not a chokepoint — the anti-chokepoint. The world's detour, and rising. Eight years of Cape economics after the 1967 Suez closure is what summoned the supertanker into existence. The VLCC is a monument to a closed canal.
  • Closed 1956 (5 months) and 1967 (8 years) — long outages permanently rewrite fleet architecture. And 2021's Ever Given proved you don't need a war: one wedged ship, 6 days, 400+ queued vessels, ~$9bn a day of held-up trade.
  • Swollen by the rewired Russian trade — the exit route for Baltic-loading barrels heading the long way to Asia.
  • Roughly halved from 2023 by the Red Sea campaign. Critically: the Saudi East-West bypass pipe discharges at Yanbu on the Red Sea — so a barrel that escapes Hormuz through it still has to pass here to reach Asia. Bypasses have chokepoints too. A strait doesn't have to close to stop working; it only has to become a place where underwriters won't quote.
  • Kazakhstan is the largest single exporter here — via the CPC pipeline that runs through Russian territory to a Russian port. A single point of failure dressed as a trade route.
  • The network breathes as one system: with Hormuz throttled, Panama ran 40–41 ships a day against a normal 34–35. Squeeze one node and pressure shows up at every other node within the month.

Where the war premium actually landed

Flat price reacted instantly and forgot fastest — futures are what you can sell at 2 a.m. on a headline. The premium that stayed was administered: war-risk insurance went from ~0.25% of hull value pre-war to quotes near 10% at the peak, settling at 3–8% — that's $3–8 million per Hormuz voyage, payable before the ship moves. Invoice-level evidence showed the war's cost landing almost entirely in the underwriters' line, even on routes that physically bypass the strait, because war risk is priced by whether your destination sits inside the Joint War Committee's listed area — not by the danger your voyage actually runs.

The shadow fleet is a caste system, not a cycle. The authors' own hypothesis — that compliant tonnage returns whenever Urals dips below the price cap — was killed by the data: the G7-linked carriage share sat in a 23–33% band while Urals swung from $56 to $112 and back. Opaque ownership and non-Western insurance are sunk investments that don't un-sink when prices dip. Two-tier tanker markets are architecture.

Part 4–5

The Hidden Violence of Options

Spot tanker rates don't move like other asset classes. They gap. Toggle between the two great spikes:

Spot VLCC rate / day 12-mo time charter ≈ $110k
March 2020: VLCC rates went from $30,000/day to $265,000/day in a week as the contango recruited ships as floating warehouses — then back to $17,000 by August. All inside five months.

Through the 2026 spike, spot printed near $470,000/day while 12-month charters held around $110,000. That gap is itself a price — of mean-reversion expectations, charterer discipline, owner refusal to sell convexity cheap, or an uncertainty band too wide to price. The working rule: take a disruption thesis seriously not when spot spikes, but when the one-year money agrees.

The window has a closing date

The tanker fleet is simultaneously the oldest in decades (VLCCs averaging 13.7 years, a fifth of the crude fleet past 20) and carrying its largest orderbook since 2011 (22% of the fleet by April 2026). Not one tanker was demolished in May — sanctioned trades pay geriatric ships too well to die. Age, full yards, and war-trade hold the market tight until roughly 2027; then ships arrive on public schedules anyone can read. Some of the best trades in shipping history came from windows. Nobody reads the closing date. Shipping has spent 200 years proving this.

Check Yourself

Three Questions

1. Why did WTI go to −$37.63 while Brent stayed positive the same afternoon?

The glut was global, but the negative price was local: WTI holders were contractually obliged to receive barrels in a landlocked town with no spare tank space. Brent, seaborne and deliverable elsewhere, never faced that wall.

2. Which premium was "stickiest" during the 2026 Hormuz crisis?

Flat price moves first and forgets fastest. The order of stickiness runs: war-risk insurance, then freight, then grade differentials. War risk is administered off an underwriters' area listing — it spiked even on routes that physically bypassed the strait.

3. A pipeline goes into "apportionment." What is the ladder telling you?

Apportionment means nominations exceeded capacity and everyone's uncommitted volumes got cut back pro rata. Barrels now bid for the next rung up, and the location differential widens to pay for it. Watch for railcars.
Your Edge

What to Take Home

  1. There is no single oil price. Every barrel sits on a ladder of deliverable prices set by what stands between it and its buyer. Flat price is the most liquid and least meaningful signal.
  2. Watch the middle, not the headline. Insurance quotes, fixtures, and basis differentials aren't commentary on a disruption — they are the disruption, measured at the till. They confirm in real time what official statistics admit a month later.
  3. Tolls vs. options. Contracted infrastructure is a bond; spare capacity is a call option. Trading-house profits are the mark-to-market on decade-old books of physical options — not lucky directional bets.
  4. Basis is telemetry. Know the marginal machine linking two prices and its cost; that cost is fair value. A blowout through it means a rung just filled — usually before journalists know.
  5. Believe the one-year money. Spot spikes are events; period rates are convictions. And every window has a closing date printed in the shipyard orderbook — public for anyone who bothers to look.
